# Shaping Lab Level 1: The Creativity Game (_101 Things to Do with a Prop_)

## Comebefores

Read the shaping section in [The Thinking of Training](../../markdown/00-theory/07-the-thinking-of-training.md) and complete [Level 1 Zen](../../markdown/level-1/zen.md) and [Level 1 Target](../../markdown/level-1/target.md) so your dog understands clicker mechanics and won't mug your treat hand.

## Where We're Going

- Step 1: Dog orients toward and investigates a novel prop without prompting.
- Step 2: Handler shapes 3 distinct interactions on one prop across separate sessions.
- Step 3: Team handles mild extinction bursts calmly by splitting criteria cleanly.
- Step 4: Dog generalizes the _"What does this do?"_ mindset across 3 brand-new props.
- Step 5: Handler puts one favorite shaped behavior on cue.

## Think About the Creativity Game

Made famous by Karen Pryor (_101 Things to Do with a Box_), this game transforms a passive dog—one who sits and stares at you waiting for commands—into an active, confident problem-solver.

There is **no wrong answer** in Step 1. Your only job as trainer is to set an ordinary box on the floor, stay silent, keep your hands still, and click tiny movements toward the box. Dogs quickly realize: _"Hey! My trainer isn't telling me what to do—I get to invent the game!"_

> [!TIP]
> **Handler Discipline: Put Your Hands Behind Your Back**
> Most shaping failures come from trainers accidentally pointing, nodding toward the box, or saying _"Look! Get it!"_ Keep your eyes softly on the box (not staring into your dog's eyes) and let your clicker be your only feedback.

## About the Cues

During Steps 1–4, **do not use a verbal cue**. Setting the prop on the floor is the invitation; picking the prop up ends the game. Only in Step 5—once a single behavior is fluent and offered eagerly—do you add a word or hand signal.

## Steps

### Step 1: Orienting and investigating a novel prop.

Choose a quiet room with non-slip flooring. Place an empty shallow cardboard box or upside-down laundry basket on the floor 2–3 feet away from you.

1. Stand or sit quietly with your clicker ready and treats out of sight in a pouch or bowl. Say nothing.
2. The instant your dog glances at the box, **Click!** and toss a treat a couple of feet _away_ from the box so she walks away to eat it and gets a fresh approach.
3. Repeat 5–6 times. Soon she will step closer to sniff or investigate the box. Click any head turn, step toward, or sniff of the box.
4. End the 2-minute session by picking up the box and putting it completely out of sight.

---

### Step 2: Shaping 3 distinct interactions on one prop.

Over three separate short sessions using the same box, raise your criteria gently so you shape **three different behaviors** (pick one goal per session):

- **Session A — Nose inside the box:** Click only when her nose breaks the plane over the top rim of the box, then lower down inside, then touching the bottom inside.
- **Session B — Front paw on/in the box:** Wait for a paw lift near the box (instead of clicking nose touches), click a single paw tap on the rim or floor of the box, then click placing one paw firmly inside.
- **Session C — Pushing or circling the box:** Click nudging the side of the box with her nose or shoulder so it slides an inch, or stepping around one side of it.

#### Try It Cold

Set the box down the next day without warm-ups. Watch how quickly your dog walks straight to the box and begins trying out her repertoire (_nose? paw? nudge?_) instead of staring at you.

---

### Step 3: Handling extinction bursts calmly.

When you stop clicking an easy behavior (like sniffing the box) because you want the next step (like touching with a paw), dogs naturally experience a mini **extinction burst**—they try harder, offer a variation, or look slightly puzzled.

Practice keeping extinction bursts constructive rather than frustrating:

1. Use the **3-second / 3-miss rule**: if you withhold a click for one step and your dog tries 3 times without hitting the next approximation, **drop your criteria immediately** on the 4th try and click something easier to rebuild momentum.
2. Never allow frantic chewing or shredding of the prop to earn clicks—if she grabs and shakes the cardboard, calmly pick the box up for a 5-second reset, or switch to a sturdier plastic tub.
3. Keep clicking **calm, deliberate offerings** rather than wild jumping or barking.

> [!NOTE]
> **Problem: My dog just sits in front of me and stares**
> If your dog won't leave your side to look at the box, you waited too long for a big step—or she is waiting for a hand signal. Toss one single treat right next to the box to get her over there, then stay silent and click the instant her eyes or nose flick back toward the box before she returns to you!

---

### Step 4: Switching props (_"What does this do?"_).

Now test whether your dog has learned the _meta-skill_ of free-shaping, rather than just memorizing one cardboard box.

Pick 3 completely different household props (for example: a plastic colander, a rolled yoga mat, and a rubber traffic cone or heavy flower pot). Run a 90-second session with each prop:

1. Place Prop 1 on the floor. See how fast you can get 5 distinct clicks for interacting with it (sniffing, pawing, pushing, stepping over). Put it away.
2. Place Prop 2 on the floor. Repeat.
3. Place Prop 3 on the floor. Your goal is for your dog to bounce happily up to a brand-new object within 5 seconds and start experimenting.

---

### Step 5: Putting one shaped behavior on cue.

Pick your dog's favorite, cleanest shaped interaction from Steps 2–4 (for example, putting one paw inside a tray, or nudging a cone across the rug).

1. Wait until she offers the behavior immediately and reliably 5 times in a row when the prop hits the floor.
2. **Add the Cue:** As she is _actively starting_ the motion (e.g., lifting her paw right onto the tray), say your chosen cue once—like **"Step In"** or **"Push"**—then **Click** as she completes it and reward.
3. After 10–15 paired reps, set the prop down, pause 1 second, give the cue **before** she moves, and click when she performs it on cue.

#### Comeafters

Once a behavior is on cue, practice presenting two different props side-by-side and asking for the cued behavior only on the matching prop, or teach a second trick in [Shaping Lab Level 2](./level-2-head-mouth-and-stationary-tricks.md).
