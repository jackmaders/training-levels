# Stationary Clicker Training, Cooperative Care & Rehab Basics

Injury recovery is actually a golden opportunity to sharpen calm impulse control, build trust, and practice **cooperative care** behaviors that make vet re-checks and medication far less stressful. Everything below can be practiced while your dog remains settled in a comfortable **Down** or **Relax** position.

## 1. Adapting Your _Training Levels_ While Resting

If you already work through the [Training Levels](../../markdown/index.md), many Level 1–4 skills require zero standing or walking:

- **Zen (Impulse Control):** Practice [Level 1 Zen](../../markdown/level-1/zen.md) (closed-fist Zen), [Level 2 Zen](../../markdown/level-2/zen.md) (open hand or treat on the floor between his paws), and [Level 3 Zen](../../markdown/level-3/zen.md) (ignoring food tossed gently near his bed until released). Keep treats right within easy neck reach so he never lunges.
- **Stationary Nose Target ("Boop"):** Adapt [Level 1 Target](../../markdown/level-1/target.md) by sitting beside your resting dog and holding your target hand **1–2 inches** from his nose—slightly left, slightly right, or touching a soft spoon or coaster held right in front of him. Never hold the target high or far away while he is recovering.
- **Focus & Relax Around Distractions:** Practice [Level 2 Relax](../../markdown/level-2/relax.md) and [Level 3 Focus](../../markdown/level-3/focus.md)—clicking and calmly feeding your dog right between his paws for staying settled while someone walks past the pen, drops keys, or knocks lightly on a table.

## 2. Cooperative Care Skills (Stress-Free Vet Visits)

Teaching your dog to voluntarily participate in handling pays huge dividends during bandage changes, suture checks, ear drops, and follow-up exams.

### Skill A: The Sustained Chin Rest ("Start Button" Behavior)

A **chin rest** gives your dog a clear way to say _"I'm comfortable, go ahead"_—and lifting his head tells you _"Pause, I need a break."_

1. **Step 1:** While your dog is lying down, hold your flat, open palm (or a soft rolled towel on your lap) an inch below his chin. If needed, hold a treat slightly past your fingers so his chin grazes your palm as he reaches. **Click** and feed.
2. **Step 2:** Shape 1–2 seconds of gentle chin weight resting on your palm before clicking. Feed while his chin stays resting on your hand.
3. **Step 3:** Add gentle, predictable touches with your _other_ hand—briefly touching an ear flap, lifting a lip, or resting a finger on his shoulder—while he maintains the chin rest. If he lifts his chin, immediately pause the touch (respecting his "stop button") and reset quietly.

### Skill B: Calm Handling & Collar Checks

Borrowing from [Level 2 Handling](../../markdown/level-2/handling.md) and [Level 3 Handling](../../markdown/level-3/handling.md), practice pairing gentle touches with calm treats right on his bed:

- Touch his collar gently $\rightarrow$ **Click** $\rightarrow$ Treat.
- Gently touch an uninjured paw or look inside an ear $\rightarrow$ **Click** $\rightarrow$ Treat.
- Hold a clean cotton ball, nail clipper, or empty syringe near him (without using it) $\rightarrow$ **Click** $\rightarrow$ Treat.

Keep arousal very low—reward soft eyes, relaxed ears, and a heavy, resting body.

### Skill C: Object Discrimination ("Show Me")

Hold two distinct, safe household objects (e.g., a wooden spoon and a plush toy) 10 inches apart right in front of your dog's nose:

1. Present only Object A, click when he gently touches it with his nose, and give the cue (e.g., **"Spoon"**).
2. Once he touches Object A reliably on cue, hold Object A and a neutral decoy together, say **"Spoon"**, and click only when his nose touches the spoon.
3. This is a completely stationary mental game that tires dogs out surprisingly quickly.

## 3. Veterinary Rehab & Passive Range of Motion (PROM)

> [!WARNING]
> **Medical Clearance Required!**
> **Never** perform physical therapy, stretching, deep massage, or Passive Range of Motion (PROM) exercises on an injured dog without explicit instruction and clearance from your veterinarian or a certified canine rehabilitation practitioner (CCRP / VMRT). Manipulating a joint too early—or beyond its safe range—can disrupt surgical repairs, tear healing ligaments, or cause pain.

If your veterinarian or rehab therapist **has** specifically prescribed **Passive Range of Motion (PROM)** as part of your recovery plan:

- **What it is:** Moving a relaxed joint smoothly through its natural flexion and extension while the dog lies comfortably on his side and does zero muscular work.
- **How to keep it calm & positive:** Have your dog settled on supportive bedding, follow your vet's exact hand placement and repetition counts, pair gentle stillness with quiet verbal praise or calm treats at the end, and **stop immediately** if your dog tenses, pulls away, or shows any sign of discomfort.
